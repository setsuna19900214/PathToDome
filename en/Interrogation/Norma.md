# Norma Interrogation

## Norma - Interrogation I

> Intelligence before completion:
> Today, you receive a meeting request from a visitor from the Public Security Bureau. She claims her model has predicted a potential threat that has yet to occur but poses extreme danger.

> Intelligence after completion:
> Norma explains that her upgraded predictive model, Moses 2.0, has detected significant anomalies behind a physical puzzle game called Reshaping the Future, which are highly likely to trigger a serious crisis. Despite insufficient evidence, she insists on investigating and requests your assistance.
> After careful consideration, you decide to accompany her to the game venue and inspect it yourself.

### Interrogation Transcript

When you push open the interrogation room door, your steps falter for just a moment. The room is the same familiar space: one desk, two chairs, smooth walls, steady lighting. Yet something feels subtly off about these ordinary furnishings.

The items on the desk have been rearranged. The recording pens have been lined up, their tips all pointing in the same direction. File boxes that were once scattered have been stacked into perfect rectangles, their edges perfectly aligned. Even the paperclip holder that's always sat haphazardly on the desk corner has been placed parallel to the edge.

It's as if someone had arrived before you and reset the entire room to match its blueprint.

Your gaze travels across the desk, and you see a woman with dark red hair. She wears a pair of thin-rimmed glasses, the lenses catching a faint glint under the lights. Her Public Security Bureau uniform is perfectly pressed without a single crease, cuffs buttoned carefully.

She's reading through some documents with her head lowered, pen tip gliding rapidly across the pages, pausing occasionally to mark something down.

When she hears the door open, she doesn't look up immediately. Only after you approach the desk does she close the final page of the documents in her hand, file them neatly into her briefcase, and zip it shut with a crisp, efficient motion.

You glance at her, then speak tentatively.

**【Advance】**

- ...Norma?

**end**

Norma: Hello, Chief.

She glances up at you, then back down at her watch. The second hand ticks past a mark with a soft click. In the quiet room, the sound is especially clear, somehow creating a sense of urgency, reminding you that no time should be wasted.

Chief: Sorry for the wait. I hope I didn't interrupt your work.

Norma: Not at all. Thank you for being punctual. We still have two minutes and fourteen seconds until the scheduled time, but I typically allow a two-to-three-minute buffer for unforeseen circumstances. We can begin now.

She pauses briefly, her tone still flat and formulaic, devoid of any unnecessary emotion.

Norma: Chief, please close the door behind you. Time is limited, and I'd prefer not to be interrupted.

Chief: ...

This is supposed to be your interrogation room, yet somehow it feels like a conference space Norma has reserved for herself. She sits behind the desk, every item already adjusted according to her preferences, while you're just a visitor who's been granted entry.

Looking at her stern face, you recall Nightingale's reminder from earlier today: A visitor from the Public Security Bureau had requested a meeting and specifically asked to use the interrogation room, which happened to be vacant today.

The reason is simple, and very Norma. This room is absolutely quiet, with no risk of irrelevant interruptions.

You've encountered this analyst from the Public Security Bureau's Forensics Division several times during the previous MBCC Mania Leakage Incident, and you've since looked into her file.

*Calm, rational, meticulous.*

*Born into a scholarly family and graduated from a top research institute in Eastside. One of the core analysts in the Public Security Bureau's Forensics Division.*

*Outstanding academic record and exceptional professional competence. Almost ruthlessly rigorous in everything she does.*

Her manner matches what you remember, but when that rationality manifests in every minute detail, the excessive orderliness becomes oddly unsettling.

You fall silent for a moment, then close the door as requested. Just as you're about to ask the usual questions—if she would want something to drink, if the room temperature is okay, if she consents to the conversation being recorded—she speaks first.

Norma: Early this morning, "Moses" predicted an imminent threat. According to the model's calculations, if this incident occurred, the Public Security Bureau would likely suffer a casualty rate of roughly 20% among its staff. This figure is significantly higher than the average casualty rate of roughly 5% for typical major incidents.

Norma: Therefore, I've determined that your intervention will be necessary.

Her voice is toneless, as if just reading out some regular calculation results. Without any preamble or embellishment, she sounds so direct it comes across as stilted and abrupt.

For a moment, no one speaks. You blink, realizing you haven't been following what she was saying at all.

**【Advance】**

- Who is Moses?

**end**

Norma: Moses was a prehistoric prophet. It's the name I gave to my large-scale intelligent predictive model.

Chief: ...What kind of threat did it predict? Does it involve Mania or Sinners?

Norma: No specific connection has been confirmed at this time.

She pauses for a moment.

Norma: But that possibility hasn't been ruled out.

You frown. If the threat hasn't been confirmed to be related to Sinners, then officially, the Public Security Bureau should investigate it first before deciding whether to involve the MBCC. Norma clearly isn't following that procedure.

Chief: Miss Norma, the MBCC could get involved. But before that, I need to confirm something. Instead of submitting a report to the Public Security Bureau requesting an investigation, you came straight to me. Is there a reason for that?

Norma is quiet, and for the first time, an awkward hesitation crosses her face.

Several hours earlier, inside the Public Security Bureau building.

The office door is ajar. Outside in the corridor, several people holding coffee cups and folders peek occasionally inside quietly and nosily. Inside, the atmosphere isn't heated yet, but there is clearly tension in the air.

Boss: Norma, this project has been terminated. Your "upgraded model" wasn't approved, and there's no need for further review.

Norma: I understand the project is currently frozen. However, the model's algorithmic framework has been updated, and its predictive accuracy has improved by 17.6%. The "threat" it detected this time is not a mere fluctuation within the margin of error.

She places a document on the desk, the pages spreading across the surface.

Norma: The complete calculation process and data sources are all here. The error rate is only 0.087‰. If the prediction holds true, the scale of the damage will at least be between 8% and 12%. The cost of investigation is far lower than the potential loss.

The man doesn't even glance at the document. He lets out a faint sigh, his weary tone clearly implying he is not interested in discussing further.

Boss: Norma, don't you know why this project was shut down in the first place? You, of all people, should understand the consequences of what happened last time.

The onlookers in the corridor quiet down slightly. Someone lets out a low "tsk." But even as her superior's impatience grows, Norma's tone remains calm.

Norma: That incident was caused by missing source data and artificial parameter bias, not flawed mathematical logic. Moses 1.0 couldn't identify human bias, but the system has now been upgraded to version 2.012 with a self-correction mechanism. If we don't start the investigation now...

The man's fingers rap sharply against the desk, cutting her off. This time, his tone turns noticeably cold.

Boss: You're asking the Public Security Bureau to deploy human resources to verify the results calculated by a project that was shut down due to lack of credibility?

Muffled laughter drifts in from the corridor. "Here she goes again." "She still hasn't given up on that thing." "Talk about stubborn..." The murmurs aren't clearly audible, but they aren't discreet either. Seeming not to hear them, Norma simply pushes the data on the desk toward the man once again.

Norma: The logical framework is all in the report. Every variable, sample source, and deduction process can be verified. All it requires is a one-off investigation.

The man's jaw tightens, as if forcing down some rising emotion. He looks down at the report, pauses briefly, then snaps it shut and throws it back heavily to the other side of the desk.

Boss: I've already told you. Your request will never be approved.

Norma is wordless for a moment, then fixes him with an unwavering stare.

Norma: ...You're letting emotion drive your decision. Humans may reach faulty conclusions due to emotional or memory bias, but Moses does not. It relies on repeated calculations and cross-verification based on evidence chain anchors.

The man furrows his brow sharply, growing even more irritated. The whispers outside have quieted down at some point. Everyone can tell that the patience of the head of the Public Security Bureau's Technology Department is running out. Everyone except Norma, who seems entirely oblivious.

Norma: This is a warning. If you ignore it, your emotional decision may lead to a catastrophic outcome...

Boss: Enough!

The man cuts her off again. This time, he doesn't bother keeping his voice down. He points straight at the door, his tone ice-cold.

Boss: Get out, now.

The crowd outside disperses quickly. Some shrug. Others wear expressions that say they saw this coming.

Norma says nothing more. She simply puts the document back into her briefcase with practiced efficiency, then turns to leave.

Norma: I'm not here on behalf of the Public Security Bureau. I came of my own accord. You know me. You know I don't lie.

Norma has put you in a difficult position. Judging by her expression, the Public Security Bureau clearly doesn't support her view or method. While the MBCC operates independently, it still cooperates frequently with the Public Security Bureau. Getting involved could easily strain that relationship.

More importantly, you know little about Norma's model. Is its prediction worth dragging the MBCC into troubled waters?

Chief: I'm sorry, Miss Norma. The incident hasn't happened yet. Without concrete evidence that it will occur, I cannot be convinced to invest resources into this either.

Norma: Moses doesn't make mistakes, as long as the input parameters are true and accurate.

Chief: ...But I know very little about this great "prophet" of yours.

Norma glances at you and rapidly performs a set of calculations concerning efficiency in her brain. Her conclusion: Without providing detailed information about the model, she can never convince you to commit resources.

Norma: What do you want to know?

Chief: How does it work, for instance?

Norma: The world is composed of measurable parameters. Chaos is simply a complex system that has yet to be deconstructed. Given a complete logical framework, the future is not unpredictable.

Chief: ...Can you put that in simpler terms?

Norma: What are you holding right now?

Chief: The air conditioner remote.

Norma: If you let go, what will happen?

...What is she getting at?

**【Advance】**

- It will fall?

**end**

Norma: Correct. You've just made a prediction.

Chief: ...

Norma: You know the remote will fall thanks to experience and observation. But that only provides a rough judgment. You cannot work out precisely the time it will hit the floor, and the exact position it will land on.

Norma: But if you quantify the process—the height, initial position, gravitational acceleration, and external forces—you can then calculate those two things, even down to the millimeter and millisecond. That is the law of mechanics.

Norma: Similar patterns exist in human society. Crime rates, for instance, can be estimated and predicted with historical data, population density, infrastructure, and social variables. Moses simply extends this calculation to an extreme scale.

Norma speaks with confidence across from you, but your mind suddenly drifts to something you saw while reviewing her file. An old document about the Public Security Bureau's early predictive model.

**【Advance】**

- If it's really this accurate... has it ever been wrong?

**end**

Something flickers in Norma's eyes.

Chief: I've heard rumors about an incident that happened at the Public Security Bureau years ago. It was apparently related to a predictive model.

Norma: You don't need to be so tactful about it.

Her tone remains unchanged, yet the pause is noticeable.

Norma: During my first year at the Public Security Bureau, I participated in a crime rate intervention pilot program targeting a high-risk area. I collected variables related to local crimes and built a predictive model. That was Moses 1.0.

Norma: After six months of trial, the prediction accuracy reached 98.72%. The Public Security Bureau reallocated patrol resources accordingly, reducing the area's crime rate by 14%.

Norma: But at the end of that year, the Bureau leadership adjusted certain patrol points and rosters without informing me. The criminals in the high-risk area migrated in response, committing violent crimes in an area marked as low-risk by the model, resulting in fifteen casualties.

You recall the Public Security Bureau's official statement on the incident: "While the model was theoretically competent, it failed to adequately factor in the complexity of human nature and social dynamics, creating risks in its application."

**【Advance】**

- Why would the Public Security Bureau make such a decision?

**end**

Norma: Because an important official was scheduled to inspect the area that week. To accommodate his visit and create the appearance of a "safe neighborhood," the Public Security Bureau administratively intervened in the patrol arrangement, even though it went against the data.

...So that's what happened. The truth never made it into the official statement. The logic behind was diluted with carefully chosen words, the cause and effect rearranged.

Norma: The model never failed. I did. I overlooked a variable that had never been properly factored into my calculations—human flaws.

It's the first time you've seen her expression change so visibly. Anger, resentment, and perhaps something even more complex flicker briefly in her eyes before vanishing.

Norma: Humans are such a fragile species. Fear, servility, and other noises that could interfere with sound judgment. They are the greatest source of chaos in this world.

Norma: When faced with pressure and self-interest, people will always choose to sacrifice the purity of logic and ignore irrefutable evidence. This calculated hypocrisy disgusts me far more than pure, natural chaos ever could. Humans are traitors to rationality itself.

Of course, you cannot agree with such an extreme, radical point of view.

Chief: It's true that emotion can lead people to make irrational choices. But that doesn't mean it's nothing but a burden.

Chief: Because emotion is also often what drives people to make choices that defy rational calculation in extreme situations—to protect others, to shoulder risks, or even to summon extraordinary strength.

Hearing your counterargument, Norma simply scoffs.

Norma: Emotions and feelings are nothing more than the outward expressions of hormonal reactions. The pathetic part is that humans cannot control them, and they make themselves slaves to them instead. The human heart is a highly unstable, highly confounding, and low-yield variable. It is the root cause of systemic disorder.

You frown. Some people may naturally be less sensitive to emotion, but she seems so angry, as if she once suffered considerable trauma because of emotion. She's trying to defend rationality, yet the words coming out of her mouth right now are clearly anything but rational.

This feels more like a trauma response, a means of self-defense... What exactly happened to her?

You don't press the argument any further. Whatever she's been through, clearly, you cannot sway her with just a few sentences, and that's not why you're here today anyway. You steer the conversation back on track.

Chief: ...So after that incident, you took the fall for the Public Security Bureau and were reassigned to the Forensics Division? I'm...

Before you can even say the word "sorry," Norma cuts you off expressionlessly. Sure enough, now that you've changed the subject, she's quickly returned to her usual composed self.

Norma: No need to pity me. It's actually for the best. The Forensics Division has shorter chains of evidence, fewer variables, and minimal social interference. It's more efficient, and so I'm more at ease.

Chief: ...Fair point. Dead people don't introduce many variables, I suppose. At least they won't suddenly move just because some bigwig from Eastside walks in.

You try to lighten the mood with a joke. Norma tilts her head slightly. She clearly doesn't get it.

Norma: Indeed, theoretically speaking, corpses do not move on their own. Even if post-mortem bloating or muscular contractions occur, the force generated is insufficient to cause displacement.

Chief: ...?

Her unironic explanation leaves you speechless. How can a person have absolutely zero sense of humor?

As Norma continues her explanation, the terminology becomes increasingly incomprehensible. Worse still, the way she's looking at you is starting to resemble how she might examine a specimen on her table. A chill runs down your spine, and you quickly go back to your original topic.

Chief: ...So what about your model? What happened to it?

Norma: Moses 1.0's model was taken down by the Public Security Bureau. All related code and data have been archived. But I was able to reconstruct the core algorithm from memory and continue refining Moses 2.0. I only have access to public data now, so the prediction accuracy has been affected, but it can still identify certain risks.

Norma: For example, this morning, the system detected an abnormal risk indicator, and its highly unlikely associated entity. A live puzzle-solving game.

Chief: ...A puzzle-solving game?

Norma switches on her laptop and turns the screen toward you. You see an ad for the game. The title stands out in bold letters: "Reshaping the Future—The Only Experience Tailored Just for You."

Norma: The model's projections indicate that if this game is allowed to continue operating, it will trigger a series of interconnected crises within a matter of months. I've reviewed all available public information. Approximately twenty-three thousand players have participated in it so far.

She taps a few keys.

Norma: But there isn't any developer information available publicly. The brand, the team, and their previous works have all been deliberately hidden. It's as if the developer knew from the very beginning that people would be looking for them, and they don't want to be found.

Norma: Furthermore, it puts a lot of emphasis on the "personalized experience" element. The story, stages, and feedback every player encounters are different. Theoretically, a product like this should generate substantial discussion about those varied experiences.

She pauses, fingertip tapping the screen.

Norma: But the reviews show the exact opposite. No one is discussing the game mechanics, narrative, or any specifics. Every single review parrots the same phrase: "An unparalleled experience." The players all seem to unanimously agree with this comment.

Chief: That doesn't sound "personalized" at all. More like... everyone's being guided toward the same conclusion.

Norma nods slightly.

Norma: I attempted to contact several players directly. Yet, regardless of how I phrased my questions, their responses were identical to the reviews. That level of consistency is inherently illogical.

You try to follow her reasoning.

Chief: So either the data were fabricated... or the players themselves were influenced?

Norma: I don't have enough information yet to reach that conclusion. But what I can confirm is that this so-called "personalized experience" cannot genuinely rely on randomness. The paths, feedback, and nodes must all be built upon existing data.

Norma: With sufficient data, a model can be built, and predictions can be made. All I need to do is enter it myself, collect enough samples, and I can reconstruct its underlying structure.

She stares at you. There isn't a hint of hesitation in her tone.

Norma: My model has never been wrong. Whether or not the MBCC decides to get involved, I'm investigating this myself. If you believe in my model, then come with me.

You don't respond immediately. Your eyes linger on those nearly identical reviews, and an uneasiness suddenly arises within you.

Chief: ...Over twenty thousand people. No matter how precisely designed something is, there's bound to be errors. There will always be people who dislike it, people who stray from the intended path, people who give a different answer. But it looks like there hasn't even been a single "anomaly."

Chief: Or... were all the "anomalies" already factored in?

Norma doesn't respond, simply watching you wordlessly.

Chief: You're right. Most so-called "personalization" is just pre-designed paths. That's why I want to see how it manages to drive so many different people's choices toward the same conclusion.

You look at her.

Chief: As for your model... I'm not entirely convinced by it yet.

Norma frowns, about to object, but you smile at her and speak first.

Chief: But I trust you. Come on, let's find out if this game is indeed free of anomalies, or if it's simply turned them into "inevitability."

## Norma - Interrogation II

You may not be good at expressing emotions, but you do care. That's what helped me understand you. That's what made me trust you.

Norma doesn't respond right away. She ponders silently, as if breaking down your words, reorganizing them, then slowly integrating them into her understanding. After a long moment, she finally begins quietly.

Norma: ...Perhaps you're right. Human emotions aren't completely pointless. In certain extreme situations, they can indeed produce... outcomes that exceed what any model could predict.

Chief: That's right. Emotions are hard to quantify and accurately incorporate into models... but they're always there. Whether you try to analyze them or not, they'll always...

You pause for a moment.

Chief: Remind you that you actually care about certain people and certain things.

Norma seems dazed for a moment. She doesn't deny it. Instead, she lets out a soft breath, and a faint, somewhat bitter smile touches her lips.

Norma: Perhaps you're right.

After a brief silence, you change the subject.

Chief: Speaking of which... what are you planning to do about Eileen? You both clearly care about each other. It'd be a shame to just leave things like this, don't you think? Why not sit down and talk it through properly?

Norma's steps falter slightly. Her gaze drifts ahead, settling on nothing in particular.

Norma: ...There's no need. I did hurt her back then. She was right. I shouldn't have imposed my logic on her. Maybe she would've been better off without me. Then she wouldn't have been so seriously traumatized. I have no right to ask for her forgiveness.

Norma: Just being able to see her again... was already a bonus. And besides...

Her voice softens slightly.

Norma: Eileen seems... better now than she was before. She was able to move on and recover. I'm glad for her.

You don't press further, only offering a soft hum of acknowledgment. As the sky turns dark, streetlights cast long shadows on the ground.

Another weekend arrives. You invite Eileen out and bring her all the way to Norma's apartment building.

Eileen: What exactly is this "fun activity" you're talking about?

Chief: Following Norma.

Eileen: Huh?! Hey, just because we had a falling out doesn't mean I'm going to help you stalk her!

Chief: Shh. Follow me.

The building door clicks open. Norma steps out, her stride as brisk as ever.

You raise your hand, signaling Eileen to follow. She stamps her feet in protest but ultimately relents and catches up.

You tail her through the streets and watch as she enters a bank. Through the glass window, the counter's light falls on Norma, illuminating her calm and focused expression.

Bank Teller: Hi, Miss Norma, good to see you again. Same as usual? Fifteen transfers to the designated accounts? Would you like me to give you an update on their financial situations?

Norma: I've already reviewed the monthly reports. Johnny's family still lacks a sustainable income source. I'll set up an online game design course tailored to his situation next month. When he comes to withdraw his cash, please try to suggest to him that he could maybe become a game designer.

Norma: Also, please pay Holly's counseling fees while you're at it.

Eileen seems surprised.

Eileen: Johnny and Holly... Could they be...

You nod, confirming her guess.

Chief: That's right. Those are the victims from that incident years ago, or their family members.

Eileen doesn't say another word, simply watching Norma. Something shifts in her gaze.

Bank Teller: Don't worry, I'll take care of it. Miss Norma, you've been anonymously helping these families for years now. You truly have a heart of gold.

Norma: Don't call me Norma. How many times do I have to tell you? I go by Cindy here.

Bank Teller: Of course, Miss Norma.

The two are clearly well acquainted. Norma shoots the teller a sharp glare, but the teller only smiles, unfazed.

Eileen: ...You brought me here so I'd see the kindness Norma never displays in front of anyone, didn't you?

Eileen lowers her gaze and sighs softly.

Eileen: At first, when the pain was at its worst... I did resent her. I hated that she couldn't just be normal for once and understand how powerless I felt back then.

Eileen: But of course I knew she only wanted to help. It's just... in that state, I couldn't meet her expectations anymore. I was terrified of disappointing her, and I hated myself for not being able to just... heal faster.

Eileen: All of it piled up, bit by bit, until it crushed me. I projected the disgust I felt for myself on Norma... But maybe, deep down, I just wanted to push her away. So my pain wouldn't affect her anymore.

Chief: Maybe what hurt her was never being affected by your pain, but that you pushed her away. You might be one of the few friends she had... perhaps her only friend. So, will you consider giving her another chance?

Eileen: Didn't I already give her a chance? But she actually locked me behind a fire door! Damn it, she still thought she was helping me...

Eileen's eyebrows shoot up, the edge returning to her voice. But she catches herself in the middle and stops. Her shoulders sag slightly as she shakes her head in resignation.

Eileen: ...Whatever. That's just how she is. Dumb and stubborn, always deciding things for others. Guess I'll just have to be more forgiving.

She lowers her head with a smile, her voice softening.

Eileen: I'll reach out to her again. She's an important friend to me. Always has been, and still is. All those memories we shared... I don't want to just let them go.

You look over at Norma.

A faint smile graces her face. She's likely already aware of those eyes on her back, yet she doesn't acknowledge it. Instead, she pretends not to notice, quietly watching some children play in the courtyard outside while continuing to fill out remittance forms at the counter.

As her pen glides over the paper, bright light filters in.

Golden sunlight falls upon her shoulders, just as it once did on the swing on that dorm balcony, all those years ago.
